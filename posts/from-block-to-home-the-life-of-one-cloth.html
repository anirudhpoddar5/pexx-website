This cloth has five lives before it reaches you. Each one leaves a mark — not as a flaw, but as evidence of the human hand that shaped it.

What you are buying is not a pattern. It is a process.

The Origin — Where it begins

Before a cloth exists, a block does.

Carved from a single piece of seasoned teak, shaped by a craftsman who may have spent weeks on a single motif, the block is the beginning of everything. In Rajasthan, some of the blocks used in studios today are older than the studios themselves. The carver's knowledge — the depth of cut, the angle of chisel, the spacing between motif elements — is not written down anywhere. It lives in the hands.

The carver's work is invisible once printing begins. But without it, nothing follows.

The Making — The act of printing

A single metre of hand block printed fabric requires between 1,500 and 2,500 individual block presses.

Each press is positioned by hand. Aligned by eye. Applied with the consistent weight of a craftsman who has learned — over years — exactly how much pressure the cloth requires. Too light, and the dye sits on the surface. Too heavy, and it bleeds past the motif edge.

This is the knowledge that cannot be encoded into a machine. It is not a setting on a dial. It is muscle memory, developed across thousands of working hours at a printing table that stands perhaps forty centimetres from the ground.

The same hands that press this block today have pressed it ten thousand times before. That repetition is not monotony. It is mastery.

The Pattern — What the process creates

What you see in the finished cloth is the accumulation of four separate passes — four different blocks, four different colours, four drying cycles between each.

The grid you are looking at took a full working day to print. One metre. One craftsman. Six hours.

Each rectangle in the repeat is stamped individually. There is no mechanical registration system. The artisan reads the cloth, judges the placement, and presses. When you look closely at a genuine block print and notice that one repeat sits very slightly off from its neighbour — that is not an error. That is proof.

The Colour — Before the block touches the cloth

Before the block touches the cloth, the colour must be prepared.

Natural dye is not chosen from a chart. It is drawn from mineral, bark, root, and earth. The ochre in this print comes from iron-rich mineral deposits local to Rajasthan. The plum from fermented indigo and bark. Each dye batch behaves slightly differently depending on temperature, humidity, and the mineral content of the water used that day.

This is why no two production runs are ever exactly the same colour. The cloth carries the chemistry of the day it was made. That is not a flaw in the process. It is the deepest form of provenance a textile can carry.

The colour in this cloth is not chosen from a Pantone chart. It is drawn from the earth beneath Rajasthan.

The Question — Not every print that looks handmade is handmade

Roller printing and screen printing can approximate the visual effect of block print. At distance, and to an untrained eye, they can be convincing.

But they cannot replicate the micro-variations in pressure that create the characteristic softness of a hand-stamped edge. They cannot replicate the slight colour shifts between passes as the dye on a block depletes and is refreshed. They cannot replicate the ghost of ink at the motif edge where the block lifted from the cloth.

These are not aesthetic choices. They are process signatures. They are what genuine handwork looks like when you know how to look.

The Home — Where the cloth lives with you

When a piece of PEXX cloth arrives with you, it carries the memory of everyone who touched it.

The carver. The dye mixer. The printer who pressed it 2,000 times that day. The inspector who passed their hand across the surface before it was folded.

Slightly irregular. Deeply intentional. Made by hands that spent a lifetime learning the weight of a block.

The colour will deepen slightly after the first wash, not fade. The weave will soften with use. The motif will remain — as clear in ten years as it is today, because the dye lives inside the fibre, not on top of it.

That is what we mean by slow. That is what we mean by handmade. That is what PEXX carries — and nothing else.

Every imperfection is a signature. Every variation is a record of the day it was made.
